The brief, the research and what follows

What is actually required, what the platform research turned up, and the plan that comes out of it.

The brief

Two days — today and tomorrow — to create a small digital product, put it on sale and earn at least one cent. Not “start earning” in the abstract, but a single recorded transaction.

Success criterion, clarified

Success means a sale on record — somebody paid. Money reaching a bank account inside the 48 hours is not required.

That distinction turned out to decide everything: no mass-market platform pays a new seller within two days, but a sale on one registers instantly.

Research

How the data was gathered

The deep-research skill expects firecrawl or exa MCP — neither is connected, so this ran on web search and direct page fetches. Part of what came back was low-grade SEO filler, so every payout figure was re-checked against the platforms' own help pages. Where a claim rests on a single source, it is marked as such.

The fork that decided everything

Gumroad holds money for at least 7 days, pays out no less than $10, and puts new sellers through an extra review on top. Every platform has delays of the same shape. Hence the clarified criterion above — without it the task is unsolvable in principle.

The option with no platform at all

A Stripe Payment Link plus a landing page: no moderation, processing fees only, money lands in your own Stripe account. The downside is zero built-in traffic.

What follows

The US clears the country blockers. Stripe Connect works, and the exclusion list — Brazil, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, Thailand — does not apply here.

US sales tax changes the arithmetic. Payhip does not handle it — selling from the US, that falls on the seller. Gumroad and Lemon Squeezy absorb it as Merchant of Record.

The real risk

The bottleneck is traffic, not building. A product with no audience makes zero sales however good it is. Two days is not enough for organic reach.

The product comes from a real asset rather than an idea invented for the occasion: 108 skills, the experience of writing them, and a working setup for syncing one config across two machines. The audience that buys this is already gathered on the Claude Code directories.

Getting ready

What is being sold, at what price, how the money is taken, and what had to exist before any of it.

The product

The 10,000 Token Tax — an account of what your own Claude Code config costs before you type a character. Every installed skill, agent and command keeps its description in the context window for the whole session, whether or not it ever fires. That is not a loading cost. That is rent.

The product is assembled out of an existing asset rather than invented from nothing: 107 skills, 38 agents and 15 commands accumulated over several months, and the measured bill for them.

The script is given away inside the article: it is short, you can read the whole of it before pointing it at your home directory, and that is the only sane way to run a stranger's script. What is paid for is the analysis — what to do with the number you get back, and the reasoning behind cutting 284 components down to 104.

Taking the money

The setup from phase 01 stands: a Stripe Payment Link plus an own landing page. No moderation, processing fees only, the sale registers instantly. The directories carry traffic but never touch payments.

A personal account is enough

For an individual account Stripe does not ask for a separate business account. What it wants is an account, not a card: the form takes the routing and account numbers of an ordinary checking account. A debit card can be added on top for Instant Payouts — minutes instead of days, at roughly 1.5%.

One constraint: the name on the account has to match the name on the Stripe account, and the account has to sit at an ordinary bank. With Chime, Cash App and prepaid accounts, ACH payouts get rejected often enough to matter.

Timing — and why it does not matter

The account activates in minutes and the Payment Link is issued straight after. The first payout arrives 7–14 days after the first payment, the standard rolling period for new US accounts. Under the criterion clarified in phase 01 the transaction itself is what counts, so the payout schedule has no bearing on the result.

The price, decided

$5 stands. This audience pays $20 a month for a Claude subscription; a five-dollar threshold is not a barrier for them, it is a signal that there is something inside. Dropping to $1 would hand Stripe a third of the revenue — $0.33 on the dollar — and cheapen the product in exchange for a conversion lift it probably would not deliver.

The incident before launch

While preparing the Payment Link it emerged that the paid report had been given away free the whole time. It was committed to the public repository Aliwers/cc-tax and plainly visible in the file list, while the “Source on GitHub” link sits on the landing page itself — putting the path to the paid content two clicks from the “Get the report” button. It also turned out GitHub Pages had been enabled for a while and the landing page was already live: the URL Stripe asks for existed, it had simply been forgotten.

The secret path is treated as burned for good. It went into the history of a public repository, and GitHub keeps objects even after a history rewrite — a force-push would have bought the feeling of safety rather than safety itself.

What changed

The report got a new secret path, and the r/ directory was taken out of git. .gitignore was rewritten as a whitelist: nothing is published by default except the script, the README and the licence. Listing prohibitions is unreliable — a forgotten file goes out silently — while the inverted policy fails in the safe direction.

The hosts were split by role: the public repository carries the free script and nothing else, while the landing page and the paid report go to Vercel. The old repository is being deleted outright, the only way to remove an object already pushed to GitHub.

Language

This log was written in Russian while the product and the landing page are in English — a buyer following “the full log of that attempt” would have hit a wall of text they could not read, from a page whose whole argument is that the attempt happens in the open. The log has been translated in full; the site is now English end to end.

What it cost

The mistake was structural rather than careless: paid and free material sat in one directory, and a single git add -A was enough for the boundary to disappear. With nothing sold yet the cost is zero — it was caught before the first sale rather than after it.

The measurement everything rests on

One number carries the product, the article headline and the cover: 9,857 tokens resident in every session before a character is typed — 7,470 for 107 skill descriptions, 1,999 for 38 agents, 388 for 15 commands. Body weight is a separate story: ~322,990 tokens across all skills, median 1,932, and a single outlier at 56,453 — continuous-learning-v2, which is also one of the half-broken ones.

The estimate is characters ÷ 4. A real tokenizer would shift the absolute figures by a few percent and reorder nothing, which is why the script has no dependency. Saying so plainly in the article matters more than precision: the first commenter will ask, and a defensible method beats a defended one.

Which skills earned their place

Almost none of them

A product about the cost of 107 skills was built essentially without them. deep-research — the obvious candidate — needs firecrawl or exa MCP and degraded to ordinary web search, exactly the failure documented in the report itself. What did the work was unglamorous: shell, Python, a headless browser for the images, and gh for the repository.

This is a finding, not an anecdote. The skills that justified their rent this session were the ones wired to something real; the prose-only ones never fired.

Where the plan bent

Self-inflicted

The report was deleted mid-rebuild. A careless rm -rf while restructuring directories took the finished file with it. Rewritten from scratch — an hour gone, no lasting damage, but the whole cost of moving fast in a directory holding the only copy.

Platform

GitHub Pages ignores files starting with _. Jekyll treats them as internal. The shared stylesheet was named _shared.css and silently never published. Renamed, plus a .nojekyll marker.

Platform

Gumroad rejected the cover twice. First because a browser screenshot carries no DPI field and the form demands 72; then because 1280×720 sits exactly on the stated minimum. Fixed by writing the density explicitly and rendering at 1920×1080.

Environment

Chrome would not upload anything to Gumroad. An extension was swallowing the file dialog. Twenty minutes lost before switching browsers; Safari worked first try. Worth reaching for sooner than a diagnosis.

Time

The plan allowed roughly nine hours across six blocks. Building the artefacts took about six and matched the estimate closely. Everything over ran on the incident from phase 01 and on platform friction — the upload dialogs, the image requirements, the account onboarding — none of which appears in any estimate and all of which is where the day actually went.

One line worth keeping

The build was never the risk. Every hour that hurt was spent on things outside the editor: a leak caused by mixing paid and free files in one directory, a browser extension, an image spec, a review queue. The plan called the traffic the danger and was right about the code — it just did not price the friction around it.

First launch

Published on 29 August. What went live, what had to be rebuilt hours before, and what the numbers are.

Stripe did not make it

The Payment Link was blocked at the last step: “Payments not yet enabled — complete required tasks to enable live payments.” The account had gone into manual review. Phase 02 recorded that activation takes minutes; for this account it did not.

The decisive detail was the calendar. It was Saturday, the deadline fell on Sunday night, and manual review for a new US account normally resolves in one or two business days — that is Monday. The plan had scheduled the fallback decision for the twelve-hours-remaining mark; waiting for it would have burned the night for a verdict that was already predictable.

Switched to Gumroad

It takes a seller immediately, hosts the file itself and mails it to the buyer. Higher fee — roughly $1.00 on a $5 sale against Stripe's $0.45 — and a seven-day payout hold, neither of which touches the success criterion agreed in phase 01: the transaction counts, not the arrival of the money.

It also removed an entire class of failure. With delivery handled by the platform, the secret path, the post-payment redirect and the whole hand-rolled delivery mechanism — the exact thing that leaked in phase 02 — simply stopped existing.

The repository, rebuilt clean

The article's central hook is a curl command that fetches the free script. It returned 404: the repository had been made private hours earlier while stopping the leak. Deleting it outright was impossible — the token carries no delete_repo scope — so the old one was renamed to cc-tax-archive-private, locking the tainted history inside a private repository, and a clean public cc-tax was created on the freed name.

A check before publishing caught what an earlier check had missed: the report still sat in refs/remotes/origin/main, the pointer to the old repository. The earlier verification had inspected the working branch and reported “clean” without saying it had looked nowhere else. The remote was detached and the objects pruned before anything was pushed.

A verification that names its own scope

Two claims of “all clear” failed the same way this weekend — once on the CDN cache, once on git history. Both were true about the thing examined and silent about the thing that mattered. Checking one URL from one Fastly node, or one branch out of three refs, is not a clean bill of health; it is one observation, and it should be reported as one.

What actually worked

Not the plan from phase 01. That plan was Stripe plus directories, and neither half carried the launch. What carried it was Gumroad plus a single article — a combination that appears nowhere in the original research, assembled in the last hours because the first path closed.

Worth naming precisely: the research was not wrong about the platforms. It was wrong about which risk would bite. It priced moderation queues on marketplaces and missed that the payment processor itself has a queue.

The most useful decision

Giving the tool away. The script is the whole script, published free and open, and the article withholds nothing. It cost the illusion of leverage and bought the only thing a stranger can act on: a number they can reproduce on their own machine in ten seconds. A teaser would have been easier to write and worth nothing.

What took longer than expected

The nine-hour estimate was accurate for building and silent about everything else. The overrun came entirely from things outside the editor: a leak caused by mixing paid and free files in one directory, a browser extension eating upload dialogs, an image spec rejecting a file twice, a payment account entering review, form state discarded between tabs.

None of that appears in any plan, and all of it is where the day went. The generalisable version: estimate the friction, not the work. The work is the part you can predict.

Three lessons that cost something to learn

Boundaries, not prohibitions

Whitelist beats blacklist for anything that separates paid from free. Listing what must not ship fails silently the moment you forget a file. The inverted rule fails in the safe direction, and it is one line of config.

Scope your claims

A check must name what it examined. Twice today “all clear” meant “clear at the one place I looked” — one CDN node out of many, one git ref out of three. Both were true and both were misleading, which is worse than being wrong out loud.

Fallbacks are calendar-driven

The trigger was set at twelve hours remaining and should have been set on the calendar. A manual review starting Saturday resolves Monday, whatever the countdown says. Waiting for the planned mark would have burned a night on a predictable answer.

Would this be worth repeating

As an exercise in shipping, yes: it produced a real tool, a real article and a real product in a day, from an asset that was sitting unused. As a way to make money, the honest answer is that the format hides the actual constraint. Forty-eight hours is enough to build anything small and nowhere near enough to build an audience — so the challenge quietly tests whether you already had one.

The version worth repeating inverts the order: spend the first day publishing something useful for free, and only then build the thing that gets sold to the people it brought. That is the same two artefacts in the opposite sequence, and it is the difference between a launch and a lottery ticket.

Still open

Stripe remains in review. If it clears, the product gets a second checkout alongside Gumroad and this log gets the comparison it could not make today — a real fee and payout schedule against a real alternative, rather than two help pages quoted side by side.
